<Tldr>
The replica is not wrong; it is earlier. Whether that matters depends on what the read is for.
</Tldr>

## Q1. Stale 7 times out of 20 on a healthy set

**Question:** why did a read on a healthy MongoDB secondary return the old value 7 times out of 20?

With `w: 1` the primary acknowledges as soon as it has applied the write itself. The secondary still has to fetch the oplog entry and apply it, which took about 1 ms (median). The test read the secondary immediately after the acknowledgement, from the same process, so in 7 of 20 cases the read arrived inside that millisecond. Lag of "almost zero" is still a window.

> **Common mistake:** testing replica freshness with a human clicking around. Human reaction time hides millisecond windows that programs hit every time.

## Q2. Fresh on PostgreSQL, 9 of 9

**Question:** why were PostgreSQL standby reads fresh 9 of 9 times without a delay, and would that hold in production?

Each read went through a new `docker exec` and `psql`, about 50 ms after the write, while the standby replays a single-row change in about 2 ms. The read simply arrived after the window. In production the application often reads within a millisecond on an existing connection, the standby is busy with its own queries, and big transactions or conflicts stretch the lag (Concept 2). The 9 of 9 says the window is short on an idle laptop, not that it is closed.

> **Common mistake:** concluding "our replica lag is zero" from a few manual checks.

## Q3. Majority acknowledged, still stale

**Question:** a write was acknowledged with `w: "majority"` in 3 ms. Why was the read on `rpb-mongo3` stale for 10 s?

A majority of three is two. The primary and `rpb-mongo2` had the write, so the acknowledgement was correct. `rpb-mongo3` was configured with `secondaryDelaySecs = 10` and applied the entry 10.02–10.22 s later. A write concern describes how many members have the write when the client hears "ok", not which ones; a read on a member outside that set can be stale.

> **Common mistake:** pairing `w: "majority"` with `readPreference: secondary` and expecting read-your-writes. Use a causally consistent session (Session 4) or `w` equal to the member count.

## Q4. Marketplace reads, stale or not

**Question:** give two kinds of reads in a marketplace that may be served stale and two that may not.

May be stale (seconds): product listing and search pages for buyers, seller analytics and dashboards, category counts, recommendation feeds.
Must be fresh: the page shown right after the user's own write (order confirmation, edited listing), payment and order status used by support, stock or balance read in order to decide a write (reserve stock, refund), anything checked for uniqueness before inserting.

> **Common mistake:** deciding per service ("the catalog service uses replicas") instead of per read. The same service has reads of both kinds.

## Q5. Monotonic reads

**Question:** what is a monotonic-read violation, and how can two replicas cause one?

A client reads a value, then reads again and gets an older value: data appears to move backwards. With two replicas, one 1 s behind and one 8 s behind, a load balancer can send the first read to the fresher replica (new status "shipped") and the second to the staler one (old status "paid"). Each replica is internally consistent; the client's sequence is not. Pinning a user to one replica, or reading from the primary after a write, prevents it; MongoDB's causally consistent sessions also guarantee monotonic reads (from the documentation).

> **Common mistake:** round-robin load balancing across replicas for a user's session without considering that their lags differ.
