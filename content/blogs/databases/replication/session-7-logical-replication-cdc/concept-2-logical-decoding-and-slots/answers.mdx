<Tldr>
A slot is a bookmark that holds WAL. Watch how much it holds, cap it, and make sure it exists on the server your consumer will connect to after a failover.
</Tldr>

## Q1. The missing transaction

**Question:** why did transaction 945 never appear in the slot, and when does a transaction's first change reach the consumer?

Transaction 945 rolled back. The decoder collects each transaction's changes in the reorder buffer and only hands them to the output plugin when it reads that transaction's COMMIT record; an ABORT record makes it discard them. So the first change of a transaction reaches the consumer **after the transaction commits**, together with all its other changes. (PostgreSQL can stream large in-progress transactions to a subscriber with `streaming = on`; the subscriber still applies them only at commit.)

> **Common mistake:** expecting CDC to show work in progress, or worrying that rolled-back writes leak to Kafka. They do not.

## Q2. Two positions

**Question:** what is the difference between `restart_lsn` and `confirmed_flush_lsn`?

`confirmed_flush_lsn` is how far the consumer has confirmed: changes committed before it will not be sent again. `restart_lsn` is the oldest WAL position the decoder must re-read to rebuild the transactions that were still open at the confirmed point; it is usually a little behind. WAL older than the minimum `restart_lsn` of all slots cannot be removed, which is why retained size is measured as `pg_current_wal_lsn() - restart_lsn`.

> **Common mistake:** measuring lag from `restart_lsn` and alarming on a slot whose consumer is fully caught up but has a long-open transaction in flight.

## Q3. A consumer down for three days

**Question:** a slot's consumer has been down for three days. What do you check, and what are your two options?

Check `active`, `wal_status`, retained bytes (`pg_current_wal_lsn() - restart_lsn`), `safe_wal_size`, and free space on the WAL volume. Then either: bring the consumer back quickly and let it catch up (it will replay three days of changes; check it has the throughput), or drop the slot to free the WAL and rebuild the consumer from a new snapshot. Dropping is the right call when the disk is close to full: a full WAL disk stops the primary for everyone.

> **Common mistake:** deleting files in `pg_wal` by hand. That corrupts the database; drop the slot instead.

## Q4. After invalidation

**Question:** after `max_slot_wal_keep_size` invalidated a slot, what must the consumer do to be correct again?

The changes between its last confirmed position and the removed WAL are gone for good, so it cannot catch up. It must drop the invalidated slot, create a new one, take a fresh consistent snapshot of the tables it mirrors (a subscription does this with its initial copy; Debezium with a snapshot), and continue streaming from the new slot. Anything derived downstream from the old stream should be reconciled against that snapshot.

> **Common mistake:** recreating the slot and continuing to stream. That skips every change made while the slot was lost.

## Q5. Slots and promotion

**Question:** why was the plain slot missing after promotion, and what two settings made the failover slot usable?

Slots are local to a server and are not part of the WAL a physical standby replays, so the promoted standby had no `cdc_plain`. The failover slot existed because the standby ran with `sync_replication_slots = on` (which also needs `wal_level = logical`, `hot_standby_feedback = on`, `primary_slot_name` and a `dbname` in `primary_conninfo`). It became readable after `synchronized_standby_slots` was reset on the new primary: the value had been copied from the old primary and named a slot that did not exist there, so the walsender waited for it forever.

> **Common mistake:** testing failover slots only by checking `synced = t` on the standby. Test a real promotion and a read on the new primary.

## Q6. What a logical slot holds back

**Question:** what does a logical slot hold back besides WAL, and what does it not hold back?

It holds `catalog_xmin`: VACUUM keeps old versions of system-catalog rows the decoder may need to interpret old WAL with the table definition that was current then. It does not set `xmin`, so ordinary user tables are vacuumed normally. A physical slot used with `hot_standby_feedback = on` is different: it reports the standby's oldest snapshot as `xmin` and holds back cleanup of every table ([Session 6](/blogs/databases/replication/session-6-queries-on-replicas/concept-2-hot-standby-feedback)).

> **Common mistake:** blaming an idle logical slot for table bloat. Look at long transactions and physical slots with feedback first.
