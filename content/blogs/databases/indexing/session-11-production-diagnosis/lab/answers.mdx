<Tldr>
Measure, rank by total, read per-call evidence, test, build online, re-measure, and stop when the work left is the work the query must do. Your numbers will differ; the reasoning should not.
</Tldr>

## Q1. Total vs mean

**Question:** in your baseline run, which statement was first by total time and which by mean time? Explain the difference with numbers.

Lab run: by total, the order detail (3,990 calls × 39.3 ms = 156.9 s, 69%); by mean, the top-SKU report (121 ms, but 188 calls = 22.8 s, 10%). The order detail ran 21 times as often. Total time is what the server spends; mean time is what one caller waits.

> **Common mistake:** ranking by mean and spending the first iteration on a report.

## Q2. Per-call evidence

**Question:** what per-call evidence told you the order-detail query was missing an index, in each engine?

| Engine | Evidence | Rows returned |
|---|---|---|
| PostgreSQL | 16,707 buffers per call (`order_items` has 16,667 pages) | 2 |
| MySQL | 2,000,077 rows examined per call, `SUM_NO_INDEX_USED` = calls | 2 |
| MongoDB | 2,000,062 docs examined per call (in the `$lookup` stage) | 1 order with 2 items |

In every engine, reads ≫ results means a scan where a lookup should be.

> **Common mistake:** looking at the plan summary only; in MongoDB it said `EXPRESS_IXSCAN`.

## Q3. The login that was never logged

**Question:** why did `auto_explain` not log the login query, and how did you find it anyway?

It took ≈ 5–7 ms, under the 20 ms `log_min_duration`. It was found by `pg_stat_statements`, which aggregates every execution regardless of duration: 6,072 calls, 12% of database time, 854 buffers per call. Then `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` by hand showed the sequential scan of `customers`.

> **Common mistake:** lowering `log_min_duration` to 0 to catch it, which logs every statement's plan.

## Q4. What hypopg did not tell you

**Question:** your `hypopg` test predicted a cost of 16.55. What did it not tell you that the real build did?

The real execution (8 buffers, 0.02 ms per call in the workload), the real size (39 MB, not the 56 MB estimate), the build time and locks (`CONCURRENTLY`, Session 8), the effect on other queries' plans (the top-SKU report did not use it until the cost model changed), and the write cost (+137 bytes of WAL per checkout).

> **Common mistake:** skipping the measured verification because the hypothetical plan looked right.

## Q5. The unused index

**Question:** why did the top-SKU report not use `order_items (order_id)` after iteration 1, and what did you change?

With `random_page_cost = 4`, 19,178 index lookups were costed above one sequential scan of `order_items`, so the planner kept the hash join (≈ 19,300 buffers, two thirds read from outside `shared_buffers`, 42–69 ms). The nested loop was really 11–14 ms (77,886 buffer hits). Setting `random_page_cost = 1.1` for the database, appropriate for SSD and a mostly cached data set, let the planner pick it; the whole workload was re-run to check no other plan got worse.

> **Common mistake:** adding yet another index for the report when the existing one was simply not chosen.

## Q6. WAL of an orders insert over time

**Question:** how did the WAL of an `orders` insert change over your checkout runs after the new indexes, and why?

Lab: 333–343 bytes before; 340–378 right after building; 415–593 while it was splitting, ≈ 60,000–200,000 inserts later; 338–353 at the end. `(customer_id, created_at)` receives each new order at a random position. Built 90% full, it first had room, then its leaves filled and split in bulk (splits are WAL-logged), then settled at 54% leaf density with 2–4 splits per 20,000 inserts, having grown from 30 to 72 MB. `order_items (order_id)` was constant at +137 bytes per checkout: increasing keys always go to the rightmost leaf.

> **Common mistake:** concluding from one run that an index is "free" or "too expensive".

## Q7. Date-range indexes

**Question:** compare the date-range index you used in PostgreSQL with MySQL's and MongoDB's: size and why.

PostgreSQL: BRIN on `created_at`, 24 kB, because the heap is in insertion order and `created_at` follows it (Session 7); it needs summarization (`autosummarize = on`) as rows arrive. MySQL: `(created_at, amount)`, 26.6 MB, a covering secondary B-tree (no BRIN in InnoDB; the clustered key is `id`). MongoDB: `{ created_at: 1, amount: 1 }`, 23.8 MB, which also makes the revenue aggregation covered (0 documents fetched). Three orders of magnitude in size for the same reports.

> **Common mistake:** assuming a technique carries over between engines unchanged.

## Q8. The reports

**Question:** after the last iteration, what would you do about the two reports, and why not another index?

They were 81% of the remaining PostgreSQL database time at 2% of calls, but each already reads only what its result needs: every item of a week's ≈ 19,000 orders, or the week's orders themselves. Another index could not shrink that set and would tax every checkout. Precompute instead: a daily summary table (units per SKU per day, revenue per day) maintained by a job or trigger, so the weekly report reads 7 rows per SKU; or a materialized view refreshed nightly; or run reports on a replica so they do not compete with checkouts.

> **Common mistake:** believing every slow query needs a new index; the course's last lesson is when it does not.
