<Tldr>
Weak levels wait, strong levels abort, and contention decides how often. Measure the failure rate on your hottest rows before raising a level.
</Tldr>

## Q1. 0% vs 69%

**Question:** why did PostgreSQL READ COMMITTED have zero failures on the hot rows, while REPEATABLE READ aborted 69%?

Both levels wait for the row lock when another transfer holds it. In READ COMMITTED the waiting `UPDATE` then continues on the newest version (re-check, Concept 2). In REPEATABLE READ the newest version was committed after the transaction's snapshot, so the `UPDATE` raises 40001. With 8 clients on 10 rows almost every transfer meets a recent commit: 51,125 of 73,693 attempts failed.

> **Common mistake:** expecting the abort rate to be "a few percent" because conflicts feel rare; on hot rows they are the norm.

## Q2. MySQL RR vs PostgreSQL RR

**Question:** why did MySQL REPEATABLE READ have zero failures where PostgreSQL REPEATABLE READ had 69%?

InnoDB never aborts because a row is newer than the read view: `UPDATE` reads and locks the newest version and applies the relative change. The transfers waited for each other and all 8,000 committed. The price is the lost-update risk for absolute writes (Concept 2), which PostgreSQL's abort protects against.

> **Common mistake:** concluding MySQL "handles concurrency better"; it gives a weaker guarantee.

## Q3. MySQL SERIALIZABLE deadlocks

**Question:** what turned MySQL SERIALIZABLE transfers into deadlocks, and which change to the transfer removes it?

The `SELECT balance` before the update became `SELECT … FOR SHARE`. Two transfers share-locked the same hot row, then both requested an exclusive lock on it for the `UPDATE`; neither could proceed, and InnoDB rolled one back (2,302 of 8,000). Reading with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE` (exclusive from the start) makes the second transfer wait at the read instead, which removes this deadlock; dropping the read when it is not needed removes it too.

> **Common mistake:** "shared locks are harmless because readers don't conflict". They conflict with the upgrade to exclusive.

## Q4. Retries still failing

**Question:** with `--max-tries=10`, 3.8% of REPEATABLE READ transactions still failed. Why don't retries always succeed on hot rows?

A retry starts a new snapshot, but the other 7 clients keep committing to the same 10 rows, so the retried transaction is likely to meet another newer commit. With ≈ 70% of attempts failing, ten failures in a row is not rare for an unlucky client. Retries need backoff (and jitter), and hot rows need fewer conflicts, not just more attempts.

> **Common mistake:** an unbounded retry loop without backoff, which adds load exactly when contention is worst.

## Q5. withTransaction cost

**Question:** what does `withTransaction()` cost in the MongoDB test, and what must the callback avoid?

All 8,000 transfers committed, but the callback ran **12,194** times (52% extra work) and the run took 8.6 s instead of 4.5 s. Because the callback can run several times, it must avoid side effects outside the database (sending emails, calling payment APIs, incrementing in-memory counters) and must recompute everything from what it reads inside the transaction.

> **Common mistake:** calling an external API inside the callback, which then runs once per attempt.

## Q6. Spread-out workload

**Question:** why did the failure rates drop below 0.3% when the same workload was spread over 10,000 accounts?

Conflicts need two concurrent transactions on the **same row**. With 8 clients choosing among 10,000 accounts the chance that a row was changed during a transaction's short lifetime is tiny: 142 of 63,006 attempts at RR, 7 of 8,000 in MongoDB, 0 deadlocks in MySQL SERIALIZABLE. The level is the same; the contention is not.

> **Common mistake:** choosing a level from a benchmark on uniform random data, then deploying it on a workload with hot rows.

## Q7. SERIALIZABLE everywhere

**Question:** a team wants SERIALIZABLE everywhere. What would you ask them to measure first?

- The **failure rate** on the hottest tables (40001 / 1213 / WriteConflict per minute), with production-like skew, not uniform data.
- Whether every transaction path has a correct **retry loop** with backoff, and no external side effects inside it.
- **Latency** with retries included (p95/p99), since aborted work is repeated.
- For PostgreSQL, **false positives** from sequential scans and page-level locks: plans of the transactions' reads.
- Which business rules actually span several rows. Often only a few transactions need SERIALIZABLE; the rest can use READ COMMITTED with relative updates or locking reads.

> **Common mistake:** raising the level globally instead of per transaction.
