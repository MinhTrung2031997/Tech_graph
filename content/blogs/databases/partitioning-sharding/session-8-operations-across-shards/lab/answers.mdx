<Tldr>
Everything that crosses shards costs more: commits become two-phase, uniqueness needs a claim, ids need a scheme, moves need a pause. A shard key that keeps the common operations on one shard avoids most of it.
</Tldr>

## Q1. Sellers 10 and 3010

**Question:** a MongoDB transaction updates sellers 10 and 3010, whose balances live on different shards. Which commit protocol runs, and which node coordinates it?

Two-phase commit: two participants. The first shard the transaction wrote to (the one holding seller 10, written first) becomes the coordinator; it records the participants, gets `prepareTransaction` from both, writes the commit decision, then commits on both. mongos counts it under `commitTypes.twoPhaseCommit`. Lab cost: median 22–23 ms against 3.1–4.5 ms on one shard.

> **Common mistake:** naming mongos or the config servers as the coordinator.

## Q2. Two shards on one worker

**Question:** on Citus, a transaction updates two shards that both live on `pt-citus-w1`. How many rows does it add to `pg_dist_transaction`, and why?

None. Citus writes both shards over the same connection to w1, so the worker commits them in one local transaction. 2PC, and its rows in `pg_dist_transaction` (one per worker), only appear when writes reach a second worker: +2 rows per transaction for sellers 8 and 1.

> **Common mistake:** assuming any multi-shard write is two-phase.

## Q3. Prepared but undecided

**Question:** what does a prepared but undecided transaction hold, and what resolves it on each engine?

Its locks and its changes, durably, on every participant: other writers to those rows or documents must wait (Citus) or get write conflicts (MongoDB) until it ends. MongoDB: the coordinator shard keeps its decision in `config.transaction_coordinators` and resumes after a failover, then commits or aborts on the participants. Citus: the coordinator's `pg_dist_transaction` rows record which prepared transactions committed; the maintenance daemon (every `citus.recover_2pc_interval`, 1 min) or `recover_prepared_transactions()` commits those and rolls back the rest.

> **Common mistake:** running `ROLLBACK PREPARED` by hand on a worker without checking the coordinator's record.

## Q4. Unique `order_no`, duplicate `_id`

**Question:** why does MongoDB refuse a unique index on `order_no` for a collection sharded by `seller_id`, and which `_id` behaviour shows the same limit?

Each shard's unique index covers only its own documents; two equal `order_no` values for different sellers go to different shards, and neither shard can see the other's. MongoDB refuses the index (CannotCreateIndex 67) rather than enforce it partly. `_id` shows what happens when it cannot refuse: its index is always unique per shard, so a second document with `_id: 1` for a seller on the other shard was accepted, and `find({ _id: 1 })` returned two documents.

> **Common mistake:** relying on `_id` uniqueness when `_id` is not the shard key.

## Q5. Claims on eight shards

**Question:** the claim-collection pattern made about half the inserts two-phase on two shards. Roughly what share would it be on eight shards?

About 7 in 8 (87.5%). The claim goes to the shard of `hash(order id)` and the order to the shard of its seller; with eight equally loaded shards, the chance they coincide is 1 in 8. The two-shard lab measured 47–52%, matching 1 − 1/2.

> **Common mistake:** extrapolating the 2-shard cost to production clusters with more shards.

## Q6. Smallest index, largest index

**Question:** which primary key kept the PostgreSQL index smallest, and why was the bulk UUIDv7 index the largest?

The bigint identity: 21 MB at 90.1% leaf density, because 8-byte keys arrive in increasing order and only the rightmost leaf is written (rightmost splits leave pages 90% full). The bulk UUIDv7 index was 40 MB at 68.2%: the insert made 423 rows per millisecond, which share a timestamp prefix and differ only in random bits, so within each millisecond they arrived in random order and caused 50/50 splits among the newest pages, on top of keys twice as wide. At one row per millisecond the same layout gave 30 MB at 90.0%.

> **Common mistake:** assuming every UUIDv7 generator is monotonic within a millisecond.

## Q7. Who waited during `block_writes`

**Question:** during `block_writes`, which traffic waited and which did not?

Writes to the moving shard group waited: the seller-8 writer's worst transactions were 1.73 s and 0.98 s, matching the 1.83 s and 1.16 s moves. Reads of the moving shard did not wait (worst 156 ms), and writes to other shards were unaffected (worst 217 ms, normal noise on the emulated cluster). The colocated `seller_balances` shard moved and was blocked with it.

> **Common mistake:** reading `block_writes` as "the cluster is read-only". Only the shard group being moved is.

## Q8. The key, and what to move off

**Question:** give the shard key you would choose for marketplace orders, and one workload you would move off the shards because it fans out under any key.

`seller_id`, made compound (`seller_id` + `created_at` or order id) if one seller could outgrow a shard. Seller dashboards hit one shard (0.61 ms, 7 docs, against 1.05 ms on two shards with hashed ids), and a seller's order, wallet and fee writes commit on one shard. Platform-wide reports filtered only by date (GMV per marketplace per day) fan out under seller, hashed id and marketplace keys alike: run them on a replica or a warehouse fed by change streams or CDC.

> **Common mistake:** choosing `marketplace` because the reports group by it: three values, at most three shards, and Shopee alone is half the data.
