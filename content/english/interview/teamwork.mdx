## Handle conflicts

How do you handle conflicts with your teammates or partners?

When handling conflicts with teammates or partners, I focus on understanding the root cause first. In most cases, conflicts come from different perspectives or priorities /praɪˈɔːr.ə.t̬i/, not personal issues.

I usually start by having an open and respectful discussion to understand their point of view. I try to listen carefully and ask questions to clarify assumptions.

Then, I share my perspective and focus on the trade-offs, especially in terms of business impact, technical feasibility /ˌfiː.zəˈbɪl.ə.t̬i/, and long-term maintainability.

If needed, we align on a balanced solution that meets both short-term and long-term goals.

And if we still can’t reach an agreement, I’m open to involving a team lead or making a decision based on team priorities.

Overall, I see conflicts as a normal part of collaboration and an opportunity to improve both the solution and team alignment.

## Conflict with a teammate

Describe a time you had a conflict with a teammate. How did you resolve it?

One time, I had a disagreement with a teammate regarding how to implement a feature.

I preferred a more structured and scalable approach, while my teammate wanted a quicker solution to meet the deadline. So initially, we had different perspectives on the trade-off between speed and maintainability.

Instead of pushing my opinion, I suggested that we sit down and walk through both approaches together. We discussed the pros and cons of each option, especially in terms of long-term impact and business priorities.

After that discussion, we agreed on a balanced solution — we delivered a simpler version first to meet the deadline, but also designed it in a way that could be improved later without major refactoring.

This not only helped us resolve the conflict effectively, but also improved how we collaborate and make decisions as a team.

From that experience, I learned that conflicts are often not about who is right or wrong, but about different priorities. The key is to communicate clearly, understand each perspective, and find a solution that balances both technical and business needs.

## Mentoring juniors

Have you ever mentored /ˈmen.tɔːr/ junior developers? What was your approach?

Yes, I have had the opportunity to mentor junior developers in my team.

My approach is to first understand their current level, both technically and in terms of how they approach problem-solving. Based on that, I guide them with the right level of support rather than giving direct answers.

I usually encourage them to think through problems step by step and ask guiding questions, so they can build their own understanding.

I also provide regular feedback through code reviews, focusing not only on correctness but also on code quality, structure, and best practices.

In addition, I share practical examples from real projects to help them understand how things work in real-world scenarios.

Over time, I have seen them improve in both their code quality and problem-solving skills, and become more confident in their work.

I believe mentoring is not just about helping others grow, but also about strengthening the overall team capability and long-term success.

## Difficult colleague

Could you tell me about a time you worked with a difficult colleague?

Yes, I once worked with a colleague /ˈkɒl.iːɡ who had a very different working style from mine.

For example, they preferred to move very quickly and focus on delivering results as soon as possible, while I tend to focus more on structure and long-term maintainability. This sometimes led to misunderstandings in how we approached tasks.

Instead of seeing it as a problem, I tried to understand their perspective and the reasons behind their approach. I also made an effort to communicate more clearly and align on expectations before starting the work.

We agreed to find a balance — in some cases, we moved quickly to meet deadlines, but we also ensured that the solution could be improved later without causing technical debt.

Over time, our collaboration improved significantly, and we were able to work more effectively by combining both speed and quality.

**I believe that working with different personalities is inevitable /ˌɪnˈev.ə.t̬ə.bəl/, and the key is to stay professional, adapt, and focus on achieving the best outcome for the team.**

## Constructive feedback

How do you give and receive constructive feedback?

When giving constructive feedback, I try to be clear, respectful, and focused on the work rather than the person.

I usually start by highlighting what was done well, then point out areas for improvement with specific examples, and suggest possible solutions. My goal is to make the feedback helpful and actionable.

I also choose the right time and tone, depending on the situation, to make sure the feedback is received positively.

When receiving feedback, I keep an open mindset and try to understand the intention behind it. I don’t take it personally, but instead see it as an opportunity to learn and improve.

If something is not clear, I ask follow-up questions to better understand the feedback and how I can apply it effectively.

**I** believe that constructive feedback is essential for both individual growth and building a strong, high-performing team.

## Role in a team

What role do you usually take in a team?

In a team, I usually take on the role of a contributor with a strong sense of ownership. I focus on delivering high-quality work while also ensuring that the overall solution is maintainable and scalable.

Beyond my own tasks, I often support the team by sharing knowledge, participating actively in code reviews, and helping to clarify technical approaches when needed.

I also try to keep good communication within the team, making sure everyone is aligned on requirements and priorities, especially when working on complex features.

In some situations, I naturally step into a mentoring role for junior developers, guiding them and helping them improve their problem-solving skills.

**I see my role not just as completing tasks, but as contributing to the team’s overall success and helping others perform better as well.**
